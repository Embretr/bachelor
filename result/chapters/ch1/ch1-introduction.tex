\section{The Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Consider a small transport business consisting of one owner-operator and one vehicle. The work is straightforward at the outset. When a customer calls with goods to be moved, the operator checks the calendar, accepts the job if time is available, and turns it down if it is not. One delivery follows another, and the day arranges itself around what comes in.

Simplicity disappears as soon as two requests collide. If two customers require their goods moved at the same hour, only one can be served. A natural response is to accept the higher-paying job and refuse the other. Suppose, however, that a third call arrives later in the day for a short delivery that would fit alongside the cheaper job that was earlier turned down. The operator who had accepted both short jobs would have earned more than the operator who accepted the single larger one, and would also have retained both customers. From this point onwards, decisions can no longer be made one call at a time. The day becomes a puzzle to be filled.

Other considerations press on the puzzle, and they fall into two kinds. The first are \emph{soft constraints}, also called priorities: the operator may prefer to avoid out-of-town runs that conflict with family commitments, retain the customer who calls every Friday, or favour jobs in a familiar area. Soft constraints shape which orders are accepted and how the day is arranged, but any of them can give way when a better job arrives. The second kind are \emph{hard constraints}, and these do not give way: the legal limit on consecutive driving hours, the capacity of the vehicle, and the customer's delivery window are rules the operator cannot break regardless of what a job pays. The operator is now optimising. Arranging a working day so that revenue is high, hard constraints are respected, and soft constraints are honoured is, in substance, an optimisation problem with multiple competing objectives.

The problem changes shape when the business grows. A second vehicle and a second driver double the calendar. Each incoming call demands not only a yes or no, but an assignment: which driver, with which vehicle, on which order. The matching has to respect everything that already mattered for one driver, together with the additional rule that two assignments cannot be made to the same driver at the same time. New soft and hard constraints also enter. One driver prefers not to start the day on the north side of the city, but will if a heavier job is waiting there. The other holds the licence required for transporting dangerous chemicals, and is the only one of the two who can take the load of paint thinner that arrives every Wednesday. The puzzle now has two columns and a richer set of pieces.

The same problem at the scale of, for instance, one hundred drivers, fifty vehicles, and a thousand orders a week no longer fits on a single desk. Companies in roughly that range, tens to hundreds of drivers and dozens of vehicles handling hundreds to thousands of orders a week, are the ones this thesis is concerned with, and they correspond to the customer base of Admmit, the Norwegian software firm that offered the thesis as a bachelor task. The owner of such a company has long since stepped out of the cab. A new role has appeared, sitting between the customers ringing in and the drivers behind the wheel, whose entire day is the puzzle: a traffic coordinator. They choose who drives what, in which vehicle, and when, and they do so against the same kinds of soft and hard constraints that mattered when there was one vehicle, only multiplied across the fleet.

Coordinators of this kind sit in transport companies across the country. In 2025, Norwegian transport companies moved approximately 252 million tonnes of goods on behalf of their customers \parencite{ssb2026godstransport}. Behind every load, a traffic coordinator made the same kind of decision the one-vehicle operator made, only at greater scale and under tighter time pressure. Those decisions add up directly on the company's bottom line: a driver assigned past the weekly hours cap turns the next load into overtime, a vehicle and its driver waiting between two assignments incur cost without producing revenue, and the heaviest workload concentrated on the same few drivers week after week produces fatigue and turnover.

At a hundred vehicles, the puzzle the one-vehicle operator could carry in their head no longer fits there. To know whether a driver is close to the weekly hours cap, the coordinator opens the timesheet. To know whether the right vehicle is free at the right hour, they check the order tool. To know whether the Friday regular has already been promised a slot, they consult their own memory. The data needed for a good plan lives in four places at once, and no view assembles it. A further part of it lives nowhere at all: which routes are difficult and who is familiar with them, which licence allows which load, which vehicle suits which job, which customer must not be refused. These are the multi-driver counterpart of the family commitments and weekly regulars that shaped the one-vehicle case. Knowledge of this kind, acquired on the job and rarely written down, is termed \emph{tacit knowledge}. It is what no system can read from a screen, and it is what makes the coordinator irreplaceable.

The software the coordinator currently operates does not close the remaining gap. The two commercial Norwegian Transport Management Systems (TMS) named by the interviewed companies, Timpex and Opter, handle order management and invoicing, but neither produces an assignment plan automatically. They scale the operator's record-keeping to a fleet, but stop there. Other companies plan from internal tools, spreadsheets, memory, or telephone calls. None of the systems in current use takes on the assignment planning itself: the decision of which driver and vehicle perform which order. That is the gap a planning system would have to fill.

What the coordinator does mentally is, in effect, an algorithm. Rules such as ``this driver is close to overtime, assign the load to another'' or ``this vehicle is too small for that pallet count, substitute it'' are applied repeatedly, yet they are never written down, compared against alternatives, or refined between shifts. A computer can apply the same rules at greater scale, hold more constraints in view simultaneously, and search through more candidate plans than a person can in comparable time. The aim is not to replace the coordinator, but to take the part of the puzzle a computer can solve and leave the part that depends on tacit knowledge to the person who carries it.


\section{The Solution}
\label{sec:solution}

This thesis concerns precisely that division of labour. Ressursplanlegger (hereafter RP) is the artefact it builds and evaluates. RP is an algorithm-assisted planning platform. An algorithm reads a company's drivers, vehicles, and orders, and proposes a plan covering a horizon set by the coordinator. The coordinator opens the proposal in a calendar-style view and reviews each assignment, free to change or remove any of them. The plan takes effect only after the coordinator approves it.

Three concerns recurred often enough across the interviews with traffic coordinators and the dialogue with Admmit to be drawn out as guiding concepts for the rest of the project. \textbf{Efficiency} is what RP must measurably improve; \textbf{Trust/control} is what RP must preserve in the coordinator's hands; \textbf{Adaptability} is what RP must support across companies. The rest of this section develops each in turn.

\subsection{Efficiency}
\label{sec:anchor-efficiency}

The owner of the hundred-vehicle business asks a simple question at the end of every week. How much overtime did the company run? How many vehicle-hours sat idle between assignments? Was the workload spread evenly across the drivers, or did the same few people carry the weight again? The information required to answer is in the company's systems, yet no single view assembles it. Efficiency is the question of whether RP makes those quantities visible, and whether, once they are visible, the coordinator can move them in the right direction: less overtime, less idle time between assignments, and a more even distribution of work across the team.

\subsection{Trust/control}
\label{sec:anchor-trust-control}

The one-vehicle operator inspected every job before accepting it. The coordinator of a hundred-vehicle fleet cannot examine a thousand jobs a week one by one, yet a system that issued plans without their approval would not be acceptable to them either. Every plan the algorithm proposes is reviewed by the coordinator before it takes effect, because trust in the algorithm grows only where control over it is real.

\subsection{Adaptability}
\label{sec:anchor-adaptability}

Two transport companies of comparable size may operate on entirely different rules. The first has ten drivers working from a single depot and plans one day at a time; the second has several dozen vehicles distributed across multiple branches and plans a week ahead. The first values keeping every driver busy above all else; the second values keeping the same crews together for repeat customers. Adaptability is the capacity of the system to fit both, with operational rules and priorities adjusted per company through configuration rather than through changes to the code.


\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The puzzle from \Cref{sec:problem} supplies the questions this thesis must answer.

\begin{quote}
To what extent can an algorithm-assisted planning platform automate the traffic coordinator's planning work in Admmit's customer companies in a way that improves resource utilization (reducing overtime, idle time between assignments, and uneven driver load) and is perceived as useful by the coordinator who runs it?
\end{quote}

The main question is one of balance. The more of the planning work RP can take on, the more it can move the company's utilization numbers and the more useful it becomes to the coordinator who runs it; if it takes on too much, or takes it on in the wrong way, it begins to erode the tacit knowledge and override authority that make the coordinator irreplaceable. The question is therefore not whether to automate, but how far automation can go before it costs more than it gives.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{SQ1}: To what extent does RP reduce overtime, idle time, and uneven driver load below the level produced by current planning practice in Admmit's customer companies?
\end{quote}

SQ1 is the Efficiency side of the balance: do the owner's end-of-week numbers come down once the coordinator works from RP rather than from the present mix of order tools, spreadsheets, and memory? It is answered in §5.1.1 against a benchmark of synthetic datasets that approximate the fleet ranges of the interviewed companies, with the comparison baseline drawn from the interview accounts of how planning is done at present.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{SQ2}: Through what kind of interface and process can the traffic coordinator review and override every algorithm-generated assignment without making the planning workflow heavier than current practice?
\end{quote}

SQ2 is the Trust/control side of the balance: how can the inspection the one-vehicle operator performed by reflex be reproduced for the coordinator of a fleet, when there are a thousand jobs a week and much of what argues for or against the algorithm's choice is knowledge the algorithm cannot read off the screen? It is answered in §5.1.2 by examining the timeline, the override controls, and the conflict view in RP.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{SQ3}: Through which configurable elements can RP serve companies that differ in operational rules, fleet size, and planning horizon, without changes to the code?
\end{quote}

SQ3 is the Adaptability question: what has to be configurable for RP to fit the ten-driver depot and the multi-branch fleet without changes to the code? It is answered in §5.1.3 against the per-company configuration of soft-constraint weights and the planning horizon set in the dashboard.


\section{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:scope}

RP fills the step between the order book and the drivers' day: the conversion of a list of orders for a given period into a concrete plan that specifies who drives what, in which vehicle, and when. The length of that period is set by the coordinator and ranges from at least a single day to a week or longer, since the companies the system has to accommodate do not all plan on the same horizon. Around that core, the system holds the information the planning step requires: which drivers are employed and what they are qualified to do, which vehicles the company has and what each can carry, and when employees are working and when they are not.

The plan itself is produced by an automated solver. The coordinator picks which solver runs and how long it is allowed to search: a tight time budget returns a quicker, less thoroughly searched plan, suited to the morning rush; a longer budget returns a more thoroughly searched one, suited to the previous evening's planning session.

The priorities the solver weighs against each other are tuned per company. The cost of overtime relative to idle time, the strength with which workload should balance across drivers, and the value of keeping a job within a driver's home region are exposed in the dashboard as configurable weights. The owner who cares most about overtime sets one weighting; the owner who cares most about even workloads sets another. The same algorithm, run on the same data, returns different plans for the two companies because the two companies have asked it to optimise different things.

Several capabilities are deliberately out of scope and warrant explicit naming, since they came up early in the project and could otherwise be assumed by default. RP is not a driver-facing application and does not push notifications to drivers; it is a planning tool, not a dispatch tool, and the message that reaches the driver's phone remains the responsibility of whatever order tool the company already operates. It does not handle billing or invoicing, which Timpex and Opter already cover, and bridging the two is a project of its own. It addresses Norwegian transport only, since the regulatory frame is national. It does not track vehicles in real time on the road; it plans the day, it does not monitor the day's execution. It does not attempt to plan the order in which a single driver should visit a list of stops, sometimes called the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP), since the question RP answers is who does what, not in which order. Finally, it does not permit the algorithm to operate without a human in the loop; this is a fixed Admmit requirement, not a configurable setting.


\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

RP was built in stages, starting from the bachelor task Admmit offered. The task came with two requirements set by Admmit on behalf of its customers: that an algorithm propose the plan, and that a human-in-the-loop workflow leave the coordinator in charge of every assignment. The shape of the planning problem itself, beyond those two requirements, was open at the start. To fill it in, the project included an outreach phase: a set of interviews with traffic coordinators in Norwegian transport companies, conducted alongside an ongoing dialogue with Admmit. From those conversations the visibility gap, the role of tacit knowledge, and the limits of the existing software described in \Cref{sec:problem} were learned, together with what coordinators would and would not accept from an algorithm.

The design and construction of RP followed a Design Science Research approach: an artefact built to address a practical problem, evaluated against the problem it was built to address, refined across iterations, and reflected on in writing. The balance the main question asks for, between automation and the coordinator's tacit knowledge, is itself difficult to calibrate from the outside; with more time the project would have run a second round of interviews and a fuller iteration cycle on the trust-and-control surface, testing the balance against operating coordinators rather than against the team's own reading of the first-round transcripts. \Cref{chap:method} describes the method and the iterations in full.
