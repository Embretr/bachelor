\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:findings}

This chapter reports how far an algorithm could take over the day's planning work, and where the coordinator's Control sets the limit. The interviews in \Cref{sec:interview-themes} say where coordinators welcome automation and where they will not give it up. The benchmark in \Cref{sec:algorithm-benchmark} reports how the available solvers perform on synthetic instances. The system description in \Cref{sec:engineering-results} shows what was built to operate within those bounds, and \Cref{sec:administrative-results} closes the chapter with the sprint record. RP was not user-tested with coordinators during the project period; implications for validation are taken up in Chapter 5.


\section{Scientific Results}
\label{sec:scientific-results}

\subsection{Interview Themes}
\label{sec:interview-findings}
\label{sec:interview-themes}

All seven interviewed companies plan the day's transport assignments by hand. Bergen Bulk Transport works from phone calls and memory. Harlem Solutions plans inside Opter, three Norlog branches plan inside Timpex, Ottem plans inside an internal order system across three departments, and Nordic Crane plans inside its own system across crane and transport divisions. None of these tools generates a plan; the matching decision is the coordinator's. The scale runs from eight to twenty drivers at the smallest operator up to around forty-five vehicles across three departments at the largest, but the matching step is the same in every case: the coordinator reads the day's orders, looks at who is available, and records the assignments by hand.

The most surprising theme across the calls was a gap in what coordinators can see about resource utilisation, the operational substance of what the thesis terms Efficiency. Overtime is handled when it surfaces, not tracked toward limits. Idle time between assignments is not visible in any of the tools the seven companies use. Load across drivers is balanced from the coordinator's memory of who has done what this week, not from a structured view.

Who articulates the demand for utilisation optimisation differs from who runs the planning. Owners and Admmit, who has worked with owners across the customer base for years, ask for utilisation visibility and an algorithm-assisted plan. Coordinators ask for speed, override authority, and a system that does not get in their way. This is the operator-vs-owner asymmetry threaded through the HITL discussion in Chapter 2; the consequences for design are picked up under \textbf{Efficiency} in \Cref{sec:efficiency}.

Two pain points were named across the seven calls: dependence on tacit knowledge, and the absence of a single capacity overview. Every coordinator carried operational knowledge no current system captures, and treated this as the reason a fully automated plan would be unsafe. No interviewed company has a single view that combines driver availability, vehicle status, and assignment load; coordinators reconstruct that picture each morning from several sources and from memory.

Sick-leave handling varies materially across the seven companies. Some coordinators describe it as a small task: Norlog Lakselv handles a sick-day in ``to-tre tastetrykk'' (two or three keystrokes), and Nordic Crane reports finishing a replanning event in about twenty-seven seconds. Others describe sick-leave as the dominant problem of their day. Norlog Tana put it directly: ``jeg sitter jo egentlig med den problemløsningen hver eneste dag'' (I sit with that problem-solving every single day). The difference depends on how many drivers are available, how flexible the routes are, and whether the routes need special competence such as scanning at Norlog Tana. Ottem describes a structurally similar problem from the order side: cancellations come in unpredictably and force the same kind of reshuffling.

Attitudes toward algorithm-assisted planning were split, not aligned. Norlog Lakselv, Norlog Mo i Rana, and Norlog Tana welcomed the idea. Harlem Solutions, Ottem, and Bergen Bulk Transport were sceptical, mainly because transport is unpredictable and they wanted to keep human judgement in the loop. Nordic Crane was partly positive, asking specifically for automatic distribution eighteen to twenty-four hours before an assignment runs, rather than full real-time automation. Even the sceptical interviewees accepted the idea of an algorithmic suggestion. Ottem framed the shared expectation most directly: ``det må være noen som sitter og tenker bak meg da'' (``there must be someone sitting and thinking behind me''). Across the seven calls, the agreement was that the system should propose a plan the coordinator can correct, not replace the coordinator.

When asked what they consider in matching a driver to an assignment, coordinators name the same criteria, in a consistent frequency order: availability and current position, experience (especially on demanding routes), driving and rest-time status (legally required), competency and licence, route familiarity (especially in northern Norway), equipment match, and overtime status toward annual limits. The list is the same across companies of different size and current system, but the weight each company places on each criterion is not. Some prioritise workload balance, others vehicle-driver continuity or route familiarity. The variation is what motivates the per-company soft-constraint weights in \Cref{tab:soft-constraints}.

\subsection{Algorithm Benchmark}
\label{sec:algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm-benchmark}

The benchmark compares solver behaviour on the same synthetic planning days. It does not re-test the visibility gap, which the interview themes establish, and it does not measure production performance. The result is narrower: given identical assignment data and identical constraint settings, it shows how the available engines perform on small, medium, and large problem instances. The engines themselves and the score hierarchy they are compared under are described in \Cref{sec:algorithm-implementation}.

The benchmark problem is a daily assignment problem with two paired resources per task. Each assignment needs a driver and a vehicle, and the pair must satisfy availability, competence, vehicle, and timing constraints. If no legal pair exists, the assignment remains unassigned with a recorded reason. The dataset generator is deterministic and pool-aware: the committed benchmark data was generated with seed 42, and assignment requirements are clamped against the employees and vehicles that exist in the pool. The benchmark therefore measures solver behaviour under tight resources and overlapping time windows, not impossible requests introduced by the generator.

The seed-42 benchmark shows a clear size boundary. On the small instance, OR-Tools CP-SAT schedules 11 of 20 assignments and proves that this is optimal; greedy schedules 10 of 20. On the medium instance, CP-SAT schedules 67 of 100 assignments and proves optimality, while greedy schedules 47. On the large instance, greedy schedules 189 of 500 assignments, while CP-SAT does not finish within the 120-second cap. The result is not that small instances are easy: the small dataset is resource-tight, with only five employees and many overlapping time windows. The result is that CP-SAT dominates greedy on small and medium instances, but does not scale to the large instance under the current solo-run setup.

The auto-router uses these engine differences in the normal solve flow. A coordinator chooses \texttt{rask}, \texttt{middels}, or \texttt{grundig}. The system then chooses an engine using the category, the problem-size bucket, the company, and the run history. This is distinct from the engine portfolio fallback behind \texttt{engineId = "auto"}, which runs the engine pool in parallel and picks a winner immediately. The normal flow is the history-based auto-router; the portfolio runner remains available for explicit advanced use.

\section{Engineering Results}
\label{sec:engineering-results}

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:system-description}
\label{sec:requirements}

RP is the implemented artefact built around the planning step identified in \Cref{sec:interview-themes}. It is a web application where the coordinator works with company-scoped planning data and asks solver engines for proposed plans. One Next.js server handles the user-facing application and the business logic; the solver engines run separately and return candidate plans to the same interface. Each company's data is separated by \texttt{companyId}, so one deployment can serve several companies without mixing their assignments, employees, vehicles, planning preferences, or optimization history. \Cref{fig:architecture} shows this structure.

The application stack is narrow by design. Next.js keeps the screens and server logic in one application. tRPC keeps calls between browser and server type-checked. Prisma gives typed access to PostgreSQL. Better Auth handles sessions, roles, and company invitations. The solver layer is kept separate because planning search is different from ordinary web application logic.

RP was specified against 58 requirements: 42 functional requirements and 16 non-functional requirements, prioritised under MoSCoW. The full registers are placed in \Cref{app:requirements-functional,app:requirements-nonfunctional}, and the traceability check is described in \Cref{sec:eval-traceability}. The workflow and algorithm results below show what those requirements became in the implemented artefact.

\subsection{Coordinator Workflow}
\label{sec:coordinator-workflow}

The coordinator workflow in RP follows the same basic rhythm as current planning work: read the day, change what needs changing, and approve the plan. The planning timeline is the main working surface because it shows the assignments in the order the coordinator has to reason about them. Other screens support that work, but they do not replace the timeline as the place where the daily plan is assembled.

Override is treated as an ordinary part of planning, not as an exception. The coordinator can move an assignment by drag-and-drop and keep working even when the change creates a warning. RP records the conflict and shows it in the timeline and in the deviation viewer; it does not block the save. This keeps Control with the coordinator while still making the system's objection visible.

The solver is exposed as a planning aid rather than as a final decision. Before running it, the coordinator chooses \texttt{rask}, \texttt{middels}, or \texttt{grundig}, which sets the time-and-quality category. The system then chooses the engine. After the run, RP shows the proposed plan with its score, violations, chosen engine, and whether the auto-router used a cold race or a warm history-based choice. The coordinator then decides whether to approve it, change it, or run the solver again.

\subsection{The Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm-implementation}

The algorithm turns the coordinator's planning problem into a set of driver-vehicle choices. The input is a planning period with assignments, employees, and vehicles. The output is a set of assignments with the employees and vehicles allocated to each one. Each possible allocation is either allowed or not allowed, and the solver searches for the set of allowed allocations that gives the best plan.

RP uses three solver engines behind the same problem format. The greedy engine reads the assignments in priority order and picks the first allowed driver-vehicle combination it finds. CP-SAT searches more broadly across possible combinations within a time limit. Timefold starts from a candidate plan and tries changes that improve it. The engines differ in how they search, but they receive the same planning data and are scored by the same rules.

Normal plan generation does not ask the coordinator to choose one of these engines. The coordinator chooses a category. The category defines an engine pool and a time cap, and the auto-router decides which engine to run from that pool. In a cold cell it races the pool and logs the completed results. In a warm cell it runs the historically strongest engine for that company, size bucket, and category. An explicit \texttt{engineId = "auto"} mode remains available as an engine-portfolio fallback, but it is not the normal coordinator flow.

Hard constraints are rules a plan must satisfy before it can be treated as feasible. They cover competence, availability, vehicle status, vehicle type, and double-booking.

Soft constraints are preferences the system should satisfy when possible. Each has a per-company weight stored as the company's default for solve runs, so the same solver can produce different plans for companies that value continuity, balance, priority, or driver preferences differently. This is the algorithmic part of Adaptability.

The score is ordered by consequence. Hard violations are ranked first, because a plan that breaks feasibility rules should not win because it looks better elsewhere. Legal working-time rules are ranked next, then business-critical quality, then preference quality. Coverage is rewarded inside the business-critical tier, so assigning more work is valuable only after the stricter rules have been handled.
