RP answers the research question at prototype level: algorithm-assisted planning can take over search and visibility work, but not the coordinator's final judgement. This chapter discusses that division of work, the boundary it creates, the company variation RP has to handle, and the claims the project can defend.


\section{What RP Demonstrates}
\label{sec:primary-findings}
\label{sec:efficiency}

RP shows that traffic planning can be partly automated without making the algorithm responsible for the final plan. The system searches possible driver-vehicle assignments, checks formal constraints, and returns a plan the coordinator can inspect. The coordinator still decides whether that plan is usable.

The value of that division is practical. Current planning makes overtime, idle gaps, workload spread, and conflicts hard to see before the plan is already made. RP brings those issues into the planning moment. The coordinator does not only receive an assignment list, but a proposal with visible reasons to accept, change, or reject it.

The interviews surfaced an asymmetry that shapes the contribution. Utilisation optimisation is articulated as a need by owners and by Admmit, not by the coordinators themselves. Coordinators ask for speed, override authority, and a system that does not get in their way. \textcite{bainbridge1983ironies} anticipates this configuration: demand for automation is articulated by those who design and own the system, while the operator inherits the residual role.

This matters because transport planning is usually changed under time pressure. A system that only reports utilisation after the day is planned arrives too late to shape the plan. RP's contribution is to make utilisation and feasibility part of the decision while there is still time to act.

The benchmark adds a narrower technical claim. It shows that the solver layer can generate alternative plans under shared constraints, and that a coordinator can choose between a quick proposal and a search that explores more alternatives depending on the time available. RP can therefore search the planning problem systematically enough to support human judgement.


\section{Where Automation Stops}
\label{sec:trust-control}

Automation stops where the plan needs judgement the data cannot carry. RP can check whether a driver is available, competent, and within working-time limits. It cannot know which route is harder in winter, which driver handles a difficult customer well, or when the day calls for an exception to the usual plan. That residue is not a gap to close with more data; it is what approval-mode automation exists to preserve.

That boundary explains why approval cannot be treated as a formality. A transport plan is not finished when the solver returns a feasible allocation. It is finished when the coordinator has judged whether the allocation fits the day. RP's Control design follows from that: the system proposes, but the coordinator must be able to change the result before it becomes the plan.

This places RP on the approval side of automation. \textcite{parasuraman2000automation} place automation on a ten-level scale. Level 5 is "system suggests, human approves". Level 6 is "system acts unless the human stops it within a time window". RP sits deliberately at level 5: nothing is dispatched until the coordinator confirms. Stopping at level 5 rather than going to level 6 makes approval the constitutive step, not an exception.

But approval-mode automation only works if the coordinator has both the time and the information to judge each proposal. RP supplies the information through conflict visibility and a readable plan. Whether coordinators will actually use that time under daily pressure is a separate question, and one this thesis cannot answer. \textcite{bainbridge1983ironies} points to a related risk: when automation handles the routine cases, the coordinator only sees the hard ones, and may lose practice on the easy ones. RP does not remove that risk.

Authority alone does not produce useful HITL. The coordinator must also be able to read what the system proposes. \textcite{lee2004trust} make the design goal explicit: not greater trust, but appropriate trust. A coordinator who accepts every plan without review, and one who reverts to fully manual planning despite a competent solver, are both failures of calibration. \textcite{hoff2015trust} refine this with a three-layer model where initial trust forms from reputation and first-week experience, and dynamic trust updates with each interaction. Early errors damage trust more than later ones, which is why the deviation viewer and post-solve summary are designed for the first-week window: they make the algorithm's reasoning legible enough that an early misalignment can be inspected rather than read as a system fault. \textcite{miller2019explanation} provides the form that legibility should take: explanations should be contrastive ("why this driver rather than that one"), selective (one or two relevant causes, not a complete derivation), and social (in the operator's vocabulary, not solver internals).

The search-and-approval split is not specific to transport. The same shape may appear in other planning domains where formal rules are clear but final acceptability depends on human judgement. Shift scheduling in healthcare is one familiar example. RP's contribution is therefore not only an artefact for transport, but a working case of how approval-mode automation can be made usable when the system shows the coordinator \textit{why} a plan is feasible, not only \textit{that} it is.


\section{Making RP Fit Different Companies}
\label{sec:adaptability}

RP becomes useful across companies when local differences can be expressed as settings, not code changes. The interviews show variation in scale, planning horizon, competence needs, route familiarity, and what each company treats as a good plan. A single fixed objective would hide those differences.

The soft-constraint weights are the clearest mechanism. They let a company emphasise continuity, workload balance, priority, transitions, or driver preferences when the solver scores candidate plans. The same assignment data can therefore produce different plans because the company has different planning priorities, not because the system has been rewritten.

This separates RP from less configurable optimisation systems, where changing priorities often means changing the model or its parameters outside the coordinator workflow. In RP, the company changes weights. \textcite{mietzner2009variability} call this kind of design a multi-tenant variability pattern: one deployed instance carries a controlled set of configuration points that distinguish customers without forking the codebase. The variability points in RP are the soft-constraint weights, the solve categories, and the company-scoped data. The engines and the HITL interface are shared.

The solve categories add a second layer of fit. A coordinator should not need to choose a solver engine or a number of seconds. Choosing between \texttt{rask}, \texttt{middels}, and \texttt{grundig} is closer to the planning situation: a rushed morning needs a quick proposal, while next-week planning can spend longer searching. The system can then choose the engine behind that category.

This is the strongest Adaptability claim RP can make. It can support different companies through company-scoped data, configurable planning preferences, and category-based solving. It is not yet evidence that every transport rule can be configured. Hard constraints, local agreements, status workflows, and exception taxonomies would need a second empirical pass before RP could claim that level of fit. Adaptability here is also distinct from scalability: the thesis does not claim RP scales to thousands of drivers, only that it can serve materially different operational rulebooks within its current scope through configuration rather than code.


\section{Methodology Reflection}
\label{sec:methodology-reflection}

Design Science Research's main strength on this project was that it forced an artefact to be built early. Building forced choices the interviews alone would have left implicit, for instance whether planning priorities should live inside the algorithm or be exposed as company configuration. Without an artefact, those questions would have stayed open.

The strength of that method is traceability. The same line runs from coordinator accounts, through requirements, into the implemented workflow and benchmark. This makes the design choices inspectable: RP did not appear as a generic optimisation tool, but as a response to a specific planning problem.

The weakness is the level of evidence. DSR validation can show that an artefact is coherent and technically plausible before deployment. It cannot show how the artefact behaves in daily operation. That is a different kind of evidence.

The next methodological step is therefore field evaluation, not another synthetic benchmark. Real planning data and coordinator review would test the same artefact at the level where the research question ultimately points.

\section{Sustainability and Professional Responsibility}
\label{sec:sustainability}
\label{sec:profession-ethics}

The Sustainability Awareness Framework helps make sense of the effects RP could have beyond technical performance \parencite{becker2015karlskrona,duboc2020requirements}. RP changes what a company can measure, what a coordinator must justify, and how driver workload becomes visible. That visibility carries different risks at different levels.

For individual drivers, the same idle-time view that helps coordinators balance work could also be used to pressure single drivers. The approval step helps limit that risk, but it cannot decide how management uses the visibility RP creates. For the wider team, plan-time visibility of workload spread can help prevent the same drivers from carrying too much work, which is a fairness gain current manual planning practice cannot offer.

At the company level, the same visibility can push planning toward filling every gap, even when slack is what keeps a transport day resilient against delays and absences. RP cannot decide where that line goes; the organisation does. Technically, configurable weights and solve categories add long-term cost: each company's choices must be documented and maintained as part of how coordinators plan, not buried in code.

Three dilemmas tie sustainability to specific design questions beyond what the dimensions name. \textit{Fairness} \parencite{mittelstadt2016algorithms}: a workload-balance term that smooths total hours can systematically advantage or disadvantage individual drivers depending on the priority weighting. \textit{Accountability} \parencite{martin2019accountability}: the coordinator who approved or overrode an assignment is the answerable party, not the algorithm or the company that built it. \textit{Working conditions} \parencite{lee2018understanding}: algorithmic management can compress task slack and tighten worker discretion in ways that erode the qualitative experience of work, even where the system itself is technically neutral.

Privacy is part of the same responsibility. Planning requires employee data such as availability, competence, working hours, vehicle assignment, and absence. That data should stay company-scoped and limited to the planning task. More detailed monitoring would need a stronger justification than this artefact provides.


\section{Deployment Path}
\label{sec:adoption}
\label{sec:recommendations}

RP should be deployed as a planning layer that sits between incoming assignments and execution, not as a replacement for the order and invoicing systems companies already use. A useful deployment connects RP to those existing systems, so coordinators do not re-enter orders, vehicles, and employees by hand.

Before any deployment claim, a controlled pilot with real planning data is the next step. The pilot should record what the solver proposes, what the coordinator changes, which solve category is used, and how many conflicts remain after review. Until that pilot has run, the product claim should stay narrow: algorithm-assisted planning with coordinator approval, configurable preferences, and visible conflicts. Claims about lower overtime, less idle time, or better workload balance need operational data, not benchmark data.
